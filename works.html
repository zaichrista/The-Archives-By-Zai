The Reach Brasserie

Abstract

The Reach Brasserie is a London bar and restaurant where I have managed day-to-day bar operations and developed a seasonal cocktail programme. My responsibilities have included service standards, suppliers and staff performance. I now lead the gradual implementation of its brand work, connecting menus, signage, content and the guest journey. The rollout is ongoing.

The Question

How can the brand promise carry through from the menu to the service?

The Investigation

Working inside the operation made delivery part of the brief. A cocktail has to be viable to prepare and sell; a menu has to help a guest choose; the identity has to make sense alongside the service. My approach connects these touchpoints instead of treating them as separate design tasks. The brand work covers signage, the menu system, content and photography planning, and the experience from arrival to the table. Implementation is being phased under my lead, with operational requirements informing how the work progresses.

What I Learned

This project demonstrates how I connect brand thinking to implementation. An idea has to survive a busy service. The seasonal cocktail programme is part of my operational experience; the wider brand rollout is still in progress. As implementation develops, a useful evaluation would examine whether guests can navigate the offering, staff can deliver it consistently and the experience gives people a reason to return.

Mandaloun

Abstract

Mandaloun is a Lebanese hospitality brand in Westfield London. Over six years I supported its content production and photoshoots, working repeatedly with the same brand long enough to understand how a hospitality identity holds together, and where it needs tending, over time rather than in a single campaign.

The Question

What does a brand need from someone who shows up for it repeatedly, not just once?

The Investigation

Most creative relationships are short: a shoot, a campaign, a handoff. Six years with Mandaloun meant something different, learning the brand's actual rhythms, not just its visual identity in the abstract, and producing content that had to keep making sense season after season rather than working once and ageing badly.

What I Learned

I learned that consistency is its own creative skill, separate from having a good idea once. A brand's photography and content have to keep being recognisably itself while still feeling current, and that only gets harder to do well the longer the relationship runs, not easier.

Brand Positioning · Independent MA Project · Hospitality Concept · 2026

Bekaa

A Mediterranean-informed listening-bar proposal exploring how shared music and repeat rituals could give a venue a distinctive position.

Abstract

Bekaa is a 2026 academic hospitality strategy project developed during my MA in Cultural and Creative Industries at King’s College London. I proposed a listening bar, cocktail space and cultural venue for South Kensington, bringing music, drinks, visual art and communal ritual into one experience. The project combines cultural and competitor research with positioning, financial planning and operational strategy.

The central idea is that attachment to a place can build through repeated experiences: a remembered song, a familiar ritual or a reason to return. I call this archival capital. For Bekaa, the framework informed a proposed focus on shared listening and familiarity over time. This is a concept and business proposal, not an operating venue or a tested retention model.

The Question

What would give people a reason to return, beyond a good first impression?

The Investigation

A visit to Bar Ideal in Athens provided a starting observation: music appeared to connect the room as a shared experience. I used that observation to develop a question for Bekaa: could listening become a central reason to visit, rather than background atmosphere? One visit is a source of inspiration, not proof of customer demand.

The proposal considered South Kensington and its nearby cultural institutions as a potential context, and explored audiences including artists, students, young professionals and international residents. It compared cocktail bars, listening bars, members’ clubs, gallery cafés and cultural venues to develop a position that brought sound, art, drinks and gathering together. Demand and audience priorities would need validation before launch.

The resulting direction was a music-first venue built around slowness, intimacy and shared presence. This informed proposed investment priorities for sound and programming, as well as marketing that reveals selected details of the experience. The commercial tension is part of the strategy: longer visits must work alongside spend, capacity and operating costs. Financial planning supports the proposal; it does not establish that those assumptions will hold in practice.

What I Learned

Bekaa shows how I move from an observation to a strategic proposition and then to experience decisions. The output is a proposed brand and business direction, with choices about location, audience, programming and communication.

The key distinction is between an appealing cultural idea and a viable business. Before launch, I would test which audience values the experience most, what it would pay for and whether the proposed offer can sustain the intended pace of a visit.

The project sharpened my interest in brands that earn familiarity over time. It also made the remaining research questions explicit: who will return, what will bring them back and what evidence would justify investment?

Independent Brand Concept · Modular Fashion · 2024

Muni

An independent modular fashion concept that positions adaptable clothing around play, choice and personal authorship.

Abstract

Muni is an independent fashion concept built around a zipper connection system I designed. The idea was to let the wearer attach, remove and recombine parts of a garment. My work brought together the product concept and a brand proposition: a wardrobe that can change with the person wearing it.

The starting tension was between wanting variety and wanting to keep clothes for longer. Muni explores whether adaptability could make those desires work together. Its sustainability ambition is a hypothesis behind the concept, rather than a measured environmental benefit.

The Question

How could adaptable clothing make keeping a wardrobe feel as appealing as changing it?

The Investigation

I began with a positioning question: how could a modular clothing concept appeal through the possibilities it offers? That led to a proposition centred on play and authorship, with the wearer taking an active role in how a garment changes.

The zipper connection system made that idea concrete. Detachable and interchangeable elements offered a way to rethink a capsule wardrobe as a set of combinations. Product and brand direction developed together: the construction had to express the promise of adaptability.

The design challenge was to make the mechanism feel desirable and understandable. A connector can demonstrate a function, but the brand still needs to explain what the wearer gains. Further development would need to test ease of use, durability, cost and whether people value the combinations in practice.

What I Learned

Muni shows how I connect a product idea to a brand proposition. The strategic choice was to lead with the pleasure of changing and personalising clothes, while treating longer use as an ambition to investigate.

The designer provides the grammar; the wearer writes the sentence. That idea helped connect the product system, the promise of choice and the language of the brand.

The next question is whether the experience delivers on the promise. Research with wearers and product testing would be needed before making claims about demand, garment lifespan or reduced waste.

Brand Refresh · Client Work · 2025

Baba G — Brand Refresh Proposal

A logo refresh proposal for a Lebanese fast-food restaurant, and what it revealed about the gap between where a brand is and where it could go.

Abstract

Baba G explored how a genuine Lebanese street-food business could translate warmth, appetite and personality into a sharper visual identity without losing its neighbourhood charm.

The Question

How do you make a neighbourhood brand feel as warm as it actually is?

The Investigation

The brief I explored was how to make the identity more expressive of the business’s warmth and personality. My proposal moved the identity toward kraft gold, terracotta, stamped packaging cues and a heavier wordmark that could hold signage, paper bags and street-food energy. The project became a study in making the distance between current brand and possible brand legible.

What I Learned

This proposal explored how a visual identity could communicate the warmth of an existing hospitality business. The mockups show a proposed direction, rather than an implemented rebrand or a measured change in customer perception.

Concept Collection · Fashion Direction · 2025

Kisses from Parikyo

A speculative collection merging French couture structure, Japanese subcultural fashion and international pattern languages.

Abstract

Kisses from Parikyo is a fashion brand concept built from cultural collision, fantasy, and movement. Merging Parisian couture, Tokyoite streetwear, and international visual cultures, the project imagines a hybrid city that does not exist on a map but feels emotionally precise.

Paris and Tokyo become more than places; they become codes of desire. One carries couture, romance, polish, and historical authority. The other carries subculture, speed, experimentation, and urban intimacy. Between them, Parikyo emerges as a fictional geography of style.

The Question

What happens when cultural references become structure rather than decoration?

The Investigation

The project began with a question: how can cultural fusion feel precise rather than generic? Paris and Tokyo are both mythologised fashion cities, but they carry different emotional weights. I wanted to place these symbolic systems into conversation rather than simply collage them together.

Kisses from Parikyo was built as a fictional world, allowing the brand to move through motifs such as lipstick marks, love letters, postmarks, hotel keys, train platforms, pearls, safety pins, silk, noir black, champagne gold, and red. These became fragments of a larger emotional archive.

The project also investigated the tension between couture and streetwear. Couture often works through control, discipline, and preciousness. Streetwear works through looseness, movement, and attitude. Kisses from Parikyo placed these languages against each other to create friction: romantic but aggressive, polished but unstable, delicate but urban.

What I Learned

Through Kisses from Parikyo, I learned that cultural fusion only works when it has emotional logic. It is not enough to place references together because they look beautiful. They have to need each other.

I also learned that world-building is one of my strongest design instincts. Once Parikyo existed as a fictional city, every design choice had somewhere to belong. Colours, silhouettes, motifs, and materials became evidence of a larger world.

Most importantly, the project taught me that cosmopolitan fashion does not have to feel rootless. Influence is inevitable. The task is to handle it with imagination, care, and specificity.

Fashion Direction · Runway Show · 2023

FW23 — Women, Studied

A runway collection exploring what it means to study a woman rather than dress one.

Abstract

Women, Studied was my first full runway collection and the first project in which I understood clothing as evidence. The collection investigates the politics of looking, being looked at, and concealment, treating the dressed body as both subject and archive.

Inspired by Venus and my mother, the work explores femininity as something observed, mythologised, protected, exposed, inherited, and performed. The title carries a double meaning: women as objects of study, and women as the ones studying the room back.

The Question

What does a woman wear when she is being looked at?

The Investigation

The investigation began with Venus, not simply as an icon of beauty, but as a figure trapped inside centuries of looking. She is adored, painted, sculpted, repeated, and consumed. She is powerful because she is beautiful, yet vulnerable because beauty makes her visible.

The second source was my mother. Where Venus brought mythology, my mother brought intimacy. The collection became a dialogue between inherited femininity and constructed femininity, between the woman as image and the woman as origin.

As the first full runway show I designed and directed, the project required creative authorship across garments, casting, styling, pacing, atmosphere, and presentation. The clothing explored concealment as an active visual language. To cover the body is not always to hide it. Sometimes what is withheld becomes louder than what is revealed.

What I Learned

Through Women, Studied, I learned that fashion can think. Clothing can carry an argument without needing to explain itself verbally. A seam, veil, cut, neckline, or silhouette can ask a question about power as clearly as a sentence can.

I also learned what it means to direct a full fashion world. A runway show is not simply a sequence of clothes; it is a controlled encounter between bodies and spectators.

Most importantly, the project taught me that femininity is not passive material. It is studied because it is powerful. It is concealed because it is watched. It is inherited because it survives.

Fashion Direction · Runway Show · 2024

SS24 — Reflections on Glamour

A collection that asked what glamour actually costs, and who has historically been allowed to claim it.

Abstract

Reflections on Glamour extends the questions first opened in Women, Studied, moving from the study of looking into the theatre of desire, power, spectacle, and exclusion. If Women, Studied treated clothing as evidence, Reflections on Glamour treats clothing as reflection: beautiful, unstable, seductive, and dangerous.

The collection draws on mid-century glamour, but refuses to preserve it cleanly. Instead, it destabilises glamour through materials and silhouettes that introduce friction. The result is more image-led than theory-led, but the argument lives inside the clothing.

The Question

What does glamour cost?

The Investigation

The investigation began with the visual language of mid-century glamour: polished femininity, cinematic beauty, controlled silhouettes, high-shine surfaces, and the mythology of the woman as spectacle. This world is seductive because it appears complete. Everything is arranged. Everything knows how it wants to be seen.

Rather than recreating this glamour, the collection sought to disturb it. The question was not how to make glamorous clothes, but how to reveal the pressure inside glamour itself. Materials and silhouettes became tools of destabilisation, interrupting the image before it could settle too comfortably.

The project also explored exclusion. Glamour always creates a border between those inside the image and those outside it. It promises transformation, but only under certain conditions. By destabilising familiar glamour codes, the collection attempted to expose this border.

What I Learned

Through Reflections on Glamour, I learned that beauty becomes more interesting when it is allowed to misbehave. Glamour does not need to be rejected in order to be critiqued. It can be inhabited, exaggerated, fractured, and turned against itself.

I also learned to trust image-led thinking. Not every project begins with theory in words. Sometimes the argument is hidden inside a fabric choice, a pose, a glare, a silhouette, or the way light touches the body.

Most importantly, the collection taught me that glamour is never innocent. It is desire arranged into form. It is power made beautiful. It is spectacle with a locked door.

Co-Creative & Marketing Direction · Oxford Fashion Gala · Joined 2024

A Voyage in Ascent — Oxford Fashion Gala

Connecting a live fashion event, its creative identity and its audience campaign across a volunteer team.

Abstract

A Voyage in Ascent brought a shared creative direction to the Oxford Fashion Gala. I joined in 2024 and served as Co-Creative Director and Co-Marketing Director, working across concept development, casting direction, set design, choreography and campaign identity.

The task extended beyond the runway: photoshoot direction, styling and promotional content needed to communicate the same idea before the event took place. My contribution was to connect the show’s creative direction to the materials that introduced it to its audience.

The Question

How can one creative concept guide both the show and the campaign?

The Investigation

The creative concept used ascent as a shared reference for styling, imagery, model direction and atmosphere. This gave the photoshoot and the live show a consistent direction. The practical challenge was translating an overall concept into decisions that different contributors could use.

The campaign brought together curated social content, collaborations and a planned posting schedule. I coordinated audience-facing assets and copy across a volunteer team in my joint creative and marketing role. Ticket sales increased between 2023 and 2024, the year I joined. A verified numerical comparison is unavailable, and I cannot isolate the campaign’s effect from other factors.

What I Learned

This work demonstrates creative briefing, coordination and delivery across multiple touchpoints. A useful concept has to guide the people producing the work, as well as make sense to the audience. My specific contribution was linking the show’s direction to a coherent campaign and helping the team deliver it. The work strengthened my ability to turn an overarching idea into clear direction for contributors across a live event and its communications.

Ethnography · Oxford BA Dissertation · 2025

Tokyo Drift: The Sonic Graffiti of the Tokyoite Underground

Three weeks of interviews and observation in Tokyo, examining how music, fashion and movement communicate belonging.

The Premise

Tokyo Drift is my 2025 Oxford BA dissertation, a 10,000-word ethnographic study developed through three weeks of fieldwork in Shibuya and Shimokitazawa. It examines how hip hop takes on local meanings through music, fashion, movement and the spaces in which people gather.

I developed the concept of sonic graffiti to describe how those experiences leave marks on identity. The study treats the body as a cultural surface: clothing, gesture and movement make shared references visible. This is academic qualitative research, not a commissioned brand study.

The Question

How do sound and style help people express belonging in Tokyo’s underground music scene?

The Method

I used club visits, participant observation, formal and semi-structured interviews, informal conversations, fieldnotes, voice memos and reflective journalling. Combining these methods let me consider what participants said alongside the settings and practices I observed.

Interviews and encounters with DJs, rappers, producers, designers and club owners offered different perspectives on music history, creative practice and belonging. These included Dabo, Coma-Chi, YESBØWY, Shing02 and Robin Rastenberger. The study focused on particular scenes and people rather than attempting to represent all of Tokyo.

I brought the material into conversation with sensory ethnography, affect theory and subcultural capital, using Schacter’s work on graffiti as a conceptual reference. My task was to build an argument across sound, clothing, movement and space while remaining attentive to their local context.

The Discovery

The study argues that this culture is actively interpreted and reshaped locally. Music, clothing and movement work together to express identity; their meaning depends on the people and places around them. Sonic graffiti is the framework I developed to describe that relationship.

For brand work, this suggests a useful research question: what do cultural references mean to the people who use them? A partnership or experience should be informed by those meanings and relationships. This is a potential application of the research, not a tested commercial recommendation from the dissertation.

The project demonstrates my ability to conduct fieldwork, connect observations and write a sustained argument. Its three-week scope and qualitative approach limit generalisation. Full research is available on request.

Original Theory · Oxford Essay · 2025

What, in Sonic Terms, Might Characterise the Opposite of Intimacy?

A theoretical framework for the engineering of disconnection through sound.

The Premise

This project investigates the sonic opposite of intimacy, arguing that it is not simply silence, distance, or absence, but an active condition of disconnection produced through sound itself. I propose the concept of sonic anti-intimacy: a mode of sonic mediation that appears to create closeness while actually fostering detachment, isolation, dominance, and social fragmentation.

If sonic intimacy is a kind of touch without physical contact, sonic anti-intimacy is presence without genuine relation. Through personal audio technologies, Muzak, algorithmic playlists, virtual assistants, and engineered silence, the project examines how sound can be used to organise, discipline, and separate bodies rather than bring them into contact.

The Question

What does the opposite of intimacy sound like?

The Method

The investigation draws on sound studies, Actor-Network Theory, theories of silence, and critiques of programmed music. It treats sound as an active social force rather than a passive background object.

The project identifies three mechanisms through which sonic anti-intimacy operates: sonic isolation, where headphones and personal listening technologies privatise shared space; sonic dominance, where background music, alarms, announcements, and virtual assistants regulate behaviour; and sonic detachment, where overexposure to sound produces numbness rather than sensitivity.

The analysis moves across examples including the Walkman, the iPod, noise-cancelling headphones, Muzak, Spotify-style mood playlists, Siri, Alexa, and Cagean silence to show how modern sonic environments simulate closeness while eroding genuine connection.

The Discovery

The project reveals that sound can disconnect as intensely as it can connect. It can comfort, but it can also discipline. It can create private worlds, but those private worlds can isolate us from social and physical environments.

Sonic anti-intimacy became a way to connect several cultural objects under one theoretical framework. Headphones, Muzak, virtual assistants, algorithmic playlists, and silence all reveal the same pattern: modern sound often performs intimacy while masking systems of distance, control, and emotional management.

Academic Essay · Sound Studies · 2025

Is Pettman Right? Sonic Intimacy, ASMR, and the Hypersexualised Ear

On whether the ear can be as choosy as the eye, and what erotic ASMR says about mediated listening.

The Premise

This project examines Dominic Pettman’s claim that sonic intimacy has particular affordances because of its intangibility, temporality, and because “the ear cannot be as choosy as the eye or the hand.”

The project agrees that sound possesses unique intimate power, but challenges the idea that the ear is entirely passive or undiscriminating. Through erotic ASMR, headphone listening, microphone technology, and the embodied voice, it argues that contemporary listening practices have made the ear more selective, curated, and technologically mediated than Pettman’s formulation allows.

The Question

Can the ear look away?

The Method

The investigation focuses on the relationship between voice, body, fantasy, and technology. Erotic ASMR becomes the central case study because it demonstrates how intimacy can be generated without physical contact.

The project analyses whispers, breath, pauses, sighs, vocal texture, binaural recording, and headphone intimacy. These sonic details blur the boundary between hearing and touch, allowing the voice to function almost as a substitute body.

Rather than treating sound as something merely received, the project studies listening as an embodied and chosen practice. ASMR listeners actively select voices, accents, scenarios, genders, intensities, and emotional tones, proving that sonic intimacy is increasingly shaped by preference, desire, and technological control.

The Discovery

The project reveals that sound does not simply enter the ear; it creates atmosphere, proximity, memory, and fantasy. A whisper, breath, or pause can produce sensations that feel almost tactile.

Technology does not merely transmit intimacy. It transforms it. Headphones, microphones, and streaming platforms reshape the relationship between voice and listener, often making sound feel closer than it would in real life.

The ear may be biologically open, but culturally and technologically, it has become increasingly selective. Contemporary sonic intimacy is negotiated between body, technology, and desire.

Academic Essay · Oxford Essay · Music & Psychoanalysis · 2024

Echoes: The Fragility of the Human Psyche

A Lacanian reading of Pink Floyd's 23-minute Echoes, visualised through Kay Sage, Yves Tanguy and Salvador Dalí.

The Premise

Echoes reads Pink Floyd’s twenty-three-minute composition as a psychoanalytic document: a long-form study of how a self is assembled, held together, and quietly threatened from within. Using Lacan’s account of the fragmented body and the mirror stage, the essay treats the track’s slow build, dissolution, and return as a structural drama of the psyche rather than simply a piece of progressive rock.

To make that drama visible, the essay pairs the music with surrealist painting. Kay Sage’s desolate architectures, Yves Tanguy’s liquid horizons, and Dalí’s soft, collapsing forms become visual analogues for what the music does to the listener: they hold coherence and disintegration in the same frame.

The Question

What happens when the structures holding the self together begin to fail?

The Method

The investigation moves section by section through Echoes, mapping its sonic architecture — the sonar ping, the gradual layering, the famous seagull-like guitar textures of the middle passage, and the final resolution — onto Lacanian structures of the self. The middle section, where melody gives way to wind and disembodied cries, is read as the composition’s encounter with the Real: the point where symbolic structure fails and something unassimilable comes through.

The paintings anchor each stage of the analysis. Sage’s empty scaffolding speaks to the ego’s fragile architecture, Tanguy’s horizonless landscapes to the loss of stable ground, and Dalí’s melting forms to the body-image coming apart. The essay treats these correspondences not as decoration but as a method: using one medium to expose the structure of another.

The Discovery

The essay taught me that long-form music can carry a psychoanalytic argument in its structure, not just in its lyrics. Echoes does not describe fragility; it performs it, building a coherent world and then letting the listener feel that world thin out beneath them.

It was also where my method first became visible to me: putting theory, sound, and image into the same room and letting each discipline interrogate the others. That instinct — reading music through painting through psychoanalysis — became the template for everything I have written since.
